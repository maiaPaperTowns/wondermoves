WonderMoves evidence brief

Why each game uses one movement, which therapy research backs that movement, how WonderMoves differs from products already on the market, and how we would price it.

WonderMoves is not a medical device and does not replace occupational or physical therapy. The studies below support the therapy approaches each game is built on. They do not yet prove that our games work; that needs our own pilot (see the last section).

The design rule: one movement per game

Each game is built around one repeated movement. Progress comes from the game world (deeper dig, new treasure), never from new directions, faster speed or precise timing.

  • Limit available actions. Hernandez et al. (CHI 2013) designed six games with children with CP and recommend that, apart from moving the avatar, "at most one other action should be available".
  • No precise timing. The same paper lists "Do not require precise timing" and low penalties for errors as core guidelines for players with CP.
  • The child's own range. Each child is calibrated first. Targets never rise above the range a caregiver or therapist approves, so finishing a level never pushes the child further.
  • Completion, not speed. A movement counts when it is completed and the child returns to rest. Nothing times out.

Game by game

Evidence levels rate the therapy approach each game is built on: strong = effective for hand function, goal achievement and self-care; moderate = effective for hand function and goals; emerging = effective for hand function only, with mixed results across reviews. The effect sizes below come from a 2025 network meta-analysis of 48 randomised trials with 1,629 children with CP (Burgess et al., Dev Med Child Neurol). Hand function is the Assisting Hand Assessment (AHA, units); goal achievement is the COPM performance score (points). All values are mean differences against control.

Treasure Reach

Meets the rule · evidence: moderate
The movement
One forward reach from a supported rest, toward a Distance sensor 25–35 cm away, then back to rest.
Sensor
Modulino Distance (time-of-flight), facing the hand.
Therapy approach
Goal-directed training: repeated practice of a real, meaningful movement toward a goal.
Evidence
Goal-directed training improved hand function (AHA +3.8) and goal achievement (COPM +2.3) in the 2025 meta-analysis. Novak et al. (2020) list goal-directed training among effective interventions for CP.
How it scales
4 reaches clear a level; 5 levels unlock a coin, gemstone, fossil, cave and chest. The reach target stays at the approved and calibrated range: in testing, level 2 is listed at 70 mm but stays at 40 mm for a child whose comfortable reach is 40 mm.
Tested
On the UNO Q with the real sensor: calibration, then 3 of 3 reaches detected (41, 38, 37 mm against a 33 mm target).

Fossil Brush

Meets the rule · evidence: emerging
The movement
One back-and-forth brushing stroke, with the Movement sensor in a chunky brush handle or a soft wrist cuff.
Sensor
Modulino Movement (6-axis IMU).
Therapy approach
Action observation: Digger shows the stroke first, then the child copies it.
Evidence
Action observation improved hand function in unilateral CP (AHA +4.9, 2025 meta-analysis), but not self-care. A 2023 network meta-analysis found a smaller, uncertain effect, so this is our least certain game. In the UP-CAT trial (Sgandurra et al., 2013), 24 children with unilateral CP who watched, then practised, goal-directed actions improved their daily use of the affected arm.
Done: the board now counts completed strokes from the swing angle (at least 8° one way, then turning back). A slow stroke and a fast stroke of the same size count the same, and each stroke clears one fixed sweep of sand. Tested with simulated gyro data: slow and fast brushing gave the same count, and a resting hand gave none.

Bubble Lift (replaced Bubble Pop)

Meets the rule · evidence: strong
Why it changed
The first version, Bubble Pop, put bubbles in different places, so the child had to aim in many directions. That is the multi-directional demand the CP research says to avoid.
The movement
One lift: from rest, raise the hand straight up. Each bubble appears directly above that hand's resting spot, always in the same place. The order is left, right, then a pair that needs both hands up together, held as long as needed.
Sensor
Brio webcam with on-device pose tracking. Only hand positions leave the vision code; no video is stored or sent.
Therapy approach
Bimanual training: practising using both hands together.
Evidence
Bimanual training was the most consistently effective approach in the 2025 meta-analysis: hand function (AHA +4.6), goal achievement (COPM +3.3) and self-care (SMD 0.39). Novak et al. (2020) also rate bimanual training as effective.
Tested
With simulated hands on the board: a half lift does not pop, a full lift does, holding up does not pop twice, and a pair needs both hands. With the real camera, tracking found the left hand in 100% of frames and the right in 57% once the camera was tilted to frame the arms.

Why a game helps: dose at home

Practice dose matters

In the meta-analysis's subgroup of trials that gave more than 25 hours of practice, goal-directed training, bimanual training and modified CIMT improved goal achievement by 2.6, 2.4 and 1.9 COPM points. The authors also describe occupational therapy home programmes as a possible "dose boost".

Home practice is hard to keep up

A 2024 systematic review links parent involvement in home programmes to better outcomes. But adherence is often low: one study reports poor adherence in 34–79% of parents, and a Malaysian survey found only 45% of caregivers followed the programme. Low self-efficacy and perceived barriers are common reasons.

Where WonderMoves fits. The games turn the movement a therapist already prescribes into something a child wants to repeat at home, and show the caregiver how much practice happened.

Interactive and virtual-reality games added to usual therapy improved upper-limb activity in a 2023 meta-analysis (SMD 0.65, 22 studies, 746 participants), but the certainty was low to very low. That is the honest status of the whole category, and why we plan a pilot.

One movement, by design

Every game follows the CP game-design rules above, instead of adapting a mainstream game.

Therapist-set limits

Calibration plus an approved maximum. The game never raises difficulty on its own.

Private, on-device AI

Pose tracking runs on the Qualcomm QRB2210 (about 8 frames a second measured). No video leaves the device.

Off-the-shelf parts

An Arduino UNO Q, three small sensor boards and a webcam, instead of custom robotics.

Proposed model (to test with families and OTs, not validated).

  • Home kit sold near hardware cost, roughly an order of magnitude below Neofect Smart Kids.
  • Family subscription for games, new levels and the caregiver dashboard, priced well below a single cash OT session per month.
  • Clinic licence so an OT can set each child's limits and review home practice between visits.

These are hypotheses. The first test is whether OTs would prescribe it and whether families keep using it after the first month.

What we still have to prove

  • Pilot with an OT: a small group of children with unilateral CP using one game at home for several weeks, measured with the AHA and COPM before and after.
  • Engagement: minutes played per week, and how many children are still playing after four weeks.
  • Safety and comfort: sensor placement, breaks, and no fatigue or pain reported.
  • Ethics: research-ethics approval before any study with children.
